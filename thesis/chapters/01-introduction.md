# Introduction

## Background and Context

Game co-development, the practice of a publisher or platform holder contracting part or all of a title's production to an external studio, has grown into a substantial segment of the video game industry. Industry market-research estimates place the global co-development services market at approximately USD 4.8 billion in 2025, with Asia-Pacific holding the largest regional share at an estimated 38.2% of revenue [@marketintelo2025gamecodev]. These figures come from vendor market-research reports rather than peer-reviewed sources and should be read as order-of-magnitude estimates, not precise measurements; segment definitions vary considerably between research firms, and comparable reports place narrower slices of the outsourcing market at under USD 2 billion for the same year. What the estimates agree on, even allowing for that uncertainty, is that co-development is a competitive, bid-driven market in which engineering service providers such as Virtuos routinely compete for a limited number of large, multi-month contracts against other studios.

For an engineering business unit (BU) operating in this market, each bid represents a significant commitment of proposal-writing effort, technical scoping time, and pricing judgement, well before any revenue is realised. The decision of which opportunities to pursue, how to price them, and how to shape the proposal to the client's stated and unstated priorities is, in most engineering-led organisations, made by combining the business development team's commercial judgement with technical leads' estimates of feasibility and cost. This is not a criticism specific to Virtuos: it is the default mode of operation across professional-services and project-based engineering firms, and it has a well-documented analogue in enterprise sales, where opportunity qualification and win-probability estimation have historically relied on sales representatives' subjective assessment of a deal's likelihood to close [@yan2015sales].

The consequence of a judgement-based process is not that it performs badly on average; experienced business development and engineering leads often have good calibrated intuition. The consequence is that the judgement is difficult to make consistent across people, difficult to audit after the fact, and difficult to improve systematically, because the reasoning behind a "this bid looks competitive" assessment is rarely written down in a form that can be checked against what actually happened once the bid was won or lost. Over enough bids, the organisation accumulates a resolved history, Won and Lost outcomes, that in principle encodes exactly the pattern the team is trying to reason about by eye: which combinations of client relationship, pricing, team fit, and competitive context tend to produce a win. In practice, that history is rarely mined systematically, and the intuitive model stays implicit, unverified, and non-transferable between people.

## Problem Statement

Virtuos' Engineering BU does not currently have a quantitative, repeatable method for estimating how competitive a given proposal is likely to be before it is submitted, or for identifying, after the fact, which factors actually distinguished its won bids from its lost ones. Bid-level decisions, including whether to pursue an opportunity at all, how aggressively to price it, and which parts of the technical narrative to emphasise, are made largely on a manual, gut-feeling basis. This creates three concrete inefficiencies that motivate this thesis:

1. **Inconsistent pursuit decisions.** Without a shared, data-grounded view of what makes a bid competitive, different business development leads may reach different conclusions about the same opportunity, and the organisation has no systematic way to reconcile those views against historical outcomes.
2. **Pricing left to intuition.** Price positioning relative to the market, one of the most consequential and most reversible levers in a proposal, is set without a structured view of how price sensitivity has actually played out across past bids, by client segment or project type.
3. **Missed revenue opportunities.** Opportunities that a quantitative read of past patterns would flag as winnable may be under-pursued, while resource-intensive proposals for structurally weak opportunities may be over-invested, because the only signal available is each individual's prior experience.

A machine-learning model trained on the BU's historical bid outcomes, paired with an explanation method that shows *why* the model believes a given bid is or is not competitive, offers a way to make the implicit pattern explicit, check it against what the BU already believes, and put it in front of decision-makers in a form they can interrogate rather than simply trust.

## Research Aim and Objectives

The aim of this thesis is to design, build, and evaluate an end-to-end decision-support pipeline that predicts the win probability of an Engineering BU proposal and explains that prediction in terms a non-technical business stakeholder can act on, and to demonstrate that pipeline as a working artefact rather than only as a theoretical proposal.

The specific objectives are to:

1. Define and justify a feature set describing a bid (client relationship history, deal origin, pricing position, team and portfolio fit, and competitive context) grounded in the Engineering BU's own documented hypotheses about what drives competitiveness.
2. Build a supervised classification pipeline that predicts the probability a proposal is won, following the CRISP-DM process [@chapman2000crispdm] end to end from data understanding through to a working artefact.
3. Apply SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [@lundberg2017unified] to the trained model to produce both a global ranking of which features actually drive its predictions and a per-bid explanation of why a specific proposal scores as it does.
4. Compare that SHAP-derived ranking systematically against the BU's own documented hypotheses about what makes a bid competitive, to surface where the two agree and, more importantly, where they diverge.
5. Package the pipeline's output as an interactive artefact, a results dashboard and a what-if bid scorer, that a BU stakeholder can use directly, and evaluate that artefact against the research questions below.

## Research Questions

**RQ1 (Feature Identification).** What bid-level features, drawn from client relationship history, deal origin, pre-proposal engagement, pricing position, team and portfolio fit, and competitive context, most effectively predict a co-development proposal's competitive outcome, and how does the model's own ranking of those features compare with the Engineering BU's documented assumptions about what matters?

**RQ2 (Model Development).** Within a CRISP-DM-structured workflow, how accurately and how robustly can a supervised classification model distinguish competitive from uncompetitive proposals under realistic constraints, specifically a modest sample size, class imbalance toward losses, and noisy, partially overlapping feature effects, and what does that accuracy imply about the ceiling on what a purely historical-pattern model can achieve for this problem?

**RQ3 (Artefact Demonstration).** How does an interactive, explanation-driven dashboard, combining aggregate model evaluation, global and per-bid SHAP explanations, and a live what-if scorer, convert a trained model's output into decision-support information that an Engineering BU stakeholder can use and trust, and what does building and exercising that artefact reveal about the practical requirements for deploying this kind of tool?

These three research questions correspond, respectively, to the data and feature-engineering stage of the pipeline, the modelling stage, and the deployment and human-facing stage, and are addressed in Chapters 4 (Design and Implementation) and 5 (Evaluation) after the methodological groundwork is laid in Chapter 3.

## Significance of the Study

This thesis is positioned as Design Science Research (DSR) in the sense articulated by Peffers et al. [@peffers2007design]: its primary contribution is a designed, demonstrable artefact that solves a stated organisational problem, evaluated against that problem, rather than a purely descriptive or explanatory study. That framing matters for how the thesis should be read and judged. The contribution is not a claim about universal truths of what wins video game co-development bids; it is a validated method, a pipeline that takes a BU's historical bid data and its documented competitiveness hypotheses and turns them into a working, explainable prediction tool, together with evidence that the method behaves correctly and produces decision-relevant output.

Prior work on win-probability modelling in adjacent business domains supports the premise that this class of problem is tractable with supervised learning, while also setting realistic expectations for its limits. Yan et al. [@yan2015sales] and Rezazadeh [@rezazadeh2020generalized] both report that enterprise sales win-propensity models trained on modest, noisy, single-organisation datasets can meaningfully outperform naive baselines, while cautioning that B2B datasets are typically small relative to consumer settings, which constrains achievable accuracy. Zahid et al. [@zahid2021predicting] extend this to tendering specifically, showing that a multi-class treatment of loss reasons (no bid, client did not pursue, lost to competition) can add decision-relevant nuance beyond a flat win and loss label. On the explanation side, Olan et al. [@olan2024enabling] document growing use of SHAP-based explainability inside supply-chain decision-support systems precisely because it lets non-technical stakeholders interrogate *why* a model produced a given output, which is the same requirement this thesis treats as central to RQ3. This thesis extends that line of work to a domain, video game co-development bidding, that does not yet have a published treatment, and does so with an explicit focus on pairing the predictive model with an interactive artefact rather than reporting model accuracy alone.

## Scope, Deviations from the Original Proposal, and Limitations

Three scoping decisions, two of which represent deliberate deviations from this thesis's original proposal, define what is and is not claimed here, and are stated plainly up front rather than left for the reader to discover in later chapters.

**Synthetic data, not real bid records.** No real, resolved Virtuos bid data was available for this thesis. The dataset used throughout is synthetically generated: feature-to-outcome relationships are encoded by the author from the Engineering BU's documented feature hypotheses, with added noise, correlation between features, interaction effects, class imbalance, and label noise so that the data is not trivially learnable (full detail and justification in Chapter 3 and Appendix A). The consequence is that this thesis demonstrates that the pipeline correctly recovers a known, encoded signal under realistic conditions; it is a validation of the method, not a discovery of what actually wins real Virtuos bids. Any number reported about feature importance or model accuracy in Chapters 4 and 5 should be read with that distinction in mind, and the artefact itself carries this caveat visibly rather than only in the written thesis.

**A single gradient-boosted model, not a Random Forest versus XGBoost comparison.** The original thesis topic specified comparing Random Forest and XGBoost as candidate classifiers. During implementation, the pipeline was instead built and evaluated around a single gradient-boosted tree ensemble (scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingClassifier`), paired with a logistic-regression surrogate used specifically to drive the artefact's interactive scorer (Chapter 4 explains why a second, linear model was needed for that component). This is a deliberate narrowing of scope, made to keep the modelling, explanation, and artefact-building effort proportionate to a single-semester thesis, and it is treated in Chapter 6 as a limitation with a concrete recommendation: a Random Forest and XGBoost comparison is identified there as the most immediately valuable piece of future work, not discarded as out of scope for the research questions themselves, since RQ2 is framed around classification accuracy and robustness rather than around any one algorithm family.

**An interactive web dashboard, not a Power BI dashboard.** The original proposal specified Power BI, refreshed through an MLOps pipeline, as the artefact's delivery mechanism. The delivered artefact is instead a self-contained, interactive web dashboard (built as HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with D3.js for charting) embedding both the results view required by RQ3 and a live, client-side what-if bid scorer, published both as a shareable Claude Artifact and as a static site hosted on GitHub Pages. This substitution was made for three practical reasons, elaborated in Chapter 3: it removes a Power BI Desktop/Service licensing dependency that would otherwise gate reviewers' ability to open the artefact at all; it allows the scorer's probability calculation to run as an exact, auditable client-side computation rather than through a Power BI data-refresh cycle; and it kept iteration speed compatible with building the artefact and the thesis in parallel. The trade-off this incurs, namely that Power BI's native enterprise deployment, row-level security, and scheduled-refresh features are not demonstrated, is treated explicitly as a limitation in Chapter 6, alongside a concrete description of what porting the current pipeline's output to Power BI would involve.

Beyond these three points, the thesis is scoped to a single business unit (Engineering) within a single organisation (Virtuos), to the specific feature set described in Chapter 3, and to a proof-of-concept level of artefact maturity: production deployment considerations such as authentication, audit logging, and integration with a live CRM are out of scope.

## Structure of the Thesis

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. **Chapter 2** reviews the literature on machine learning for bid- and sales-opportunity prediction, explainable AI and SHAP in business decision support, and Design Science Research and CRISP-DM as methodological frameworks, situating this thesis's contribution relative to that work. **Chapter 3** sets out the methodology in full: the Design Science Research process followed, the CRISP-DM stages as applied to this project, the synthetic data generation methodology and its justification, and a fuller account of the three scoping deviations introduced in Section 1.6. **Chapter 4** describes the design and implementation of the pipeline and artefact: the feature engineering, the model training and SHAP explanation pipeline, and the architecture of the interactive dashboard and scorer. **Chapter 5** reports the evaluation: quantitative model performance (AUC, Brier score, precision, recall, F1), the SHAP-versus-hypothesis comparison, and a qualitative assessment of the artefact against RQ3. **Chapter 6** discusses what the results mean, the limitations introduced by the synthetic-data and scoping decisions, and the threats to validity. **Chapter 7** concludes with a summary of the contribution and a concrete set of recommendations for future work, led by moving the pipeline onto real bid data. Appendices provide the full synthetic-data generation methodology, the complete feature glossary used throughout the artefact, and selected code listings.
