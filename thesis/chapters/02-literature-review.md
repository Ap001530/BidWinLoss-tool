# Literature Review

This chapter reviews four bodies of work that this thesis draws on: machine learning applied to sales- and bid-opportunity prediction; tree-based ensemble classifiers and the trade-offs between them; explainable AI, specifically SHAP, as a mechanism for turning a model's output into something a business stakeholder can act on; and Design Science Research together with CRISP-DM as the methodological scaffolding for building and evaluating the artefact. The chapter closes by identifying the specific gap this thesis addresses: the combination of these four threads has not, to the author's knowledge, been applied to bid-competitiveness prediction in the video game co-development industry.

## Machine Learning for Sales and Bid-Opportunity Prediction

Predicting whether a commercial opportunity will be won is a well-established problem in enterprise sales analytics, and the closest published analogues to this thesis's problem come from that literature rather than from the game industry specifically. Yan et al. [@yan2015sales], working with IBM Research on data from a top-500 enterprise, frame the task as win-propensity prediction: estimating the probability that an open sales opportunity will close within a given time window. Their central methodological point is one this thesis inherits directly: business-to-business (B2B) opportunity data is small relative to consumer-scale datasets, noisy, and drawn from a market that shifts over the period the data covers, all of which constrain how much accuracy any model can realistically achieve and argue against treating a single point accuracy figure as the measure of success. The same constraint applies, likely more severely, to a single engineering BU's resolved bid history, which is the premise this thesis's synthetic-data approach is built to test under controlled conditions (Chapter 3).

Rezazadeh [@rezazadeh2020generalized] reports a generalised ensemble-modelling workflow for B2B sales prediction built on Microsoft Azure Machine Learning and evaluated on a consulting firm's historical opportunity data, with a data-enrichment and feature-engineering stage treated as integral to the pipeline rather than as a preprocessing afterthought. This supports the structural choice made in this thesis's own pipeline (Chapter 4) to treat feature engineering as its own CRISP-DM stage rather than folding it into model training. Zahid et al. [@zahid2021predicting] extend the basic win/loss framing by splitting losses into three distinct classes, no bid, customer did not pursue, and lost to competition, and report 85% accuracy with an average AUC of 0.94 for B2B tendering data once class imbalance is handled. Their multi-class treatment is a useful pointer for future work (Chapter 7): the dataset generator built for this thesis already distinguishes Won from Lost but, consistent with their finding that loss *reasons* carry separate decision-relevant information, does not yet separate "we chose not to bid" from "we bid and lost," a distinction the Engineering BU would likely want in a production version of this tool.

Two methodological lessons recur across this literature and shape how this thesis evaluates its own model in Chapter 5. First, raw accuracy is a poor summary statistic for a class-imbalanced win/loss problem, because a model that always predicts the majority class ("Lost") can post misleadingly high accuracy while never identifying a single win; He and Garcia [@he2009learning], in their survey of learning from imbalanced data, make the same point in general terms and recommend threshold-independent measures such as the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and probability-calibration measures such as the Brier score as more informative summaries, both of which this thesis reports alongside accuracy for that reason. Second, every one of these papers works with real but *confidential* organisational data that cannot be published or independently re-examined, which is a structural problem for reproducibility in this research area generally and is one of the reasons, discussed fully in Chapter 3, that this thesis instead builds and publishes a synthetic dataset whose generating process is fully disclosed.

## Tree-Based Ensemble Classifiers for Tabular Prediction Problems

Bid-level features of the kind described in Section 1.3, a mixture of categorical fields (client segment, project type), ordinal fields (relationship history), and continuous fields (price position, effort), is a textbook tabular-data classification problem, and the dominant family of models for this problem class remains tree-based ensembles rather than deep learning. Breiman's Random Forest [@breiman2001random] averages many decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap-resampled subset of the data and a random subset of features at each split, trading a small amount of bias for a substantial reduction in variance relative to a single tree; it is robust to irrelevant features, requires comparatively little hyperparameter tuning, and handles the mix of feature types in this problem natively, which is why it was specified as a candidate algorithm in this thesis's original proposal (Section 1.6).

Gradient boosting, introduced by Friedman [@friedman2001greedy], takes a different approach to the same underlying idea of combining many weak learners: rather than averaging independently trained trees, it fits each new tree to the residual error of the ensemble built so far, so that the ensemble is built as a stagewise, additive approximation that directly minimises a chosen loss function. XGBoost [@chen2016xgboost] is an engineering-optimised implementation of gradient boosting that adds regularisation terms to the objective, a more careful treatment of missing values, and substantial training-speed improvements, and was the second algorithm specified in this thesis's original proposal. In practice, Random Forest and gradient-boosted variants (including XGBoost and scikit-learn's own `GradientBoostingClassifier`) tend to perform comparably on small-to-medium tabular datasets, with the gap between them typically smaller than the gap between either and a well-tuned linear model on genuinely non-linear data, and smaller again than the variance introduced by dataset size and noise. This thesis's choice to build around scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingClassifier`, a direct implementation of Friedman's [-@friedman2001greedy] algorithm, rather than running the originally proposed Random Forest-versus-XGBoost comparison, is a scoping decision made for the reasons given in Section 1.6, not a claim that gradient boosting is definitively superior for this problem; Chapter 7 identifies the comparison as the most immediately valuable piece of future work precisely because the literature does not settle the question in advance.

A second, independent reason tree ensembles rather than linear models are the right default for this problem is that bid competitiveness plausibly involves interaction effects rather than a purely additive combination of features, for example a weak project-portfolio match may matter far more for a brand-new client than for a client with a strong prior relationship. Tree-based models capture such interactions natively through their split structure, where a linear or logistic model would require those interaction terms to be specified by hand in advance. This consideration directly motivated the synthetic data generator's design (Chapter 3), which deliberately encodes several such interaction effects so that the modelling pipeline has a genuine reason to need a non-linear learner rather than a toy problem a linear model could solve equally well.

## Explainable AI and SHAP in Business Decision Support

A model that outputs a win probability without any account of *why* is of limited use to a bid-review meeting: a business development lead who is told "62%" and nothing else has no way to check the number against what they already know, challenge it, or act differently because of it. This is the motivation for pairing the predictive model with an explanation method rather than treating prediction as the end goal, which is the premise behind RQ3 (Section 1.4).

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), introduced by Lundberg and Lee [@lundberg2017unified], addresses this by adapting the Shapley value, a concept from cooperative game theory that fairly distributes a total payoff among players based on their marginal contribution across all possible coalitions, to the problem of attributing a model's prediction to its input features. For a single prediction, SHAP assigns each feature a signed contribution such that the contributions sum exactly to the difference between that prediction and the model's average prediction across the training data; this additive, exact-decomposition property is what distinguishes it from earlier feature-importance methods, which typically report only a global ranking with no guarantee that per-instance explanations are internally consistent. For tree ensembles specifically, Lundberg and Lee's TreeSHAP algorithm computes these exact Shapley values in polynomial rather than exponential time, which is what makes SHAP computationally practical for the hundreds of predictions this thesis's pipeline explains (Chapter 4) rather than only a handful.

Published applications of SHAP in business decision-support contexts remain comparatively recent but are growing. Olan et al. [@olan2024enabling] review the use of explainable AI, with SHAP as a central example, inside supply-chain decision-support systems, and report that explainability has been instrumental in enabling organisations to act on model output with confidence rather than treating it as an opaque score; their framing of SHAP's role, making a model's reasoning available for human review rather than only its output, is the same framing this thesis adopts for RQ3 and for the dashboard design in Chapter 4. The pattern observed across this literature, including Olan et al.'s own account, is that published SHAP applications concentrate in a small number of domains (credit risk, supply chains, retail cross-selling, and clinical prediction) and that bid- or proposal-level win prediction in any industry, let alone video game co-development specifically, does not yet appear in this literature. That absence is itself part of the gap this thesis addresses.

This thesis's use of SHAP serves two distinct purposes that the literature above generally treats separately. The first is per-bid explanation: showing a decision-maker which features pushed a specific proposal's predicted win probability up or down, which is the direct analogue of Olan et al.'s supply-chain use case. The second, more specific to this thesis's design, is using SHAP's *global* feature-ranking output as a structured object to compare against the Engineering BU's own documented hypotheses about what drives competitiveness (RQ1), turning the explanation method into an instrument for checking a model against domain expertise rather than only for explaining the model's output to an end user. This comparative use of SHAP, model-derived ranking against a priori domain hypotheses, is the thesis's clearest point of methodological contribution relative to the reviewed literature, and is developed fully in Chapter 3's discussion of the hypothesis-comparison design and evaluated in Chapter 5.

## Design Science Research and CRISP-DM as Methodological Frameworks

This thesis is framed as Design Science Research (DSR), a research paradigm concerned with building and evaluating artefacts that address an identified organisational problem, as distinct from behavioural research, which seeks to explain or predict phenomena without building anything. Hevner et al. [@hevner2004design] set out seven guidelines for DSR in information systems research, of which three are directly load-bearing for how this thesis is structured: the research must produce a viable, purposeful artefact (Guideline 1); the artefact must be rigorously evaluated to demonstrate its utility (Guideline 3); and the research process itself, not only its output, must be clearly and repeatably documented (Guideline 7). Chapters 4 and 5 of this thesis are organised directly around the first two of these guidelines, describing the artefact's construction and then its evaluation as distinct, sequential activities, and the full disclosure of the synthetic-data generation process in Chapter 3 and Appendix A is this thesis's response to the third.

Peffers et al. [@peffers2007design] operationalise DSR into six concrete process steps: problem identification and motivation, definition of objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication. This thesis follows that sequence structurally: Chapter 1 covers problem identification and motivation and the definition of objectives; Chapter 3 (methodology) and Chapter 4 (design and implementation) cover design and development; Chapter 4's description of the working dashboard and scorer constitutes demonstration; Chapter 5 covers evaluation; and this thesis document itself, together with the published artefact, is the communication step. Peffers et al.'s framework explicitly allows for a "problem-centred" entry point, where the research begins from a known organisational problem rather than from an opportunity noticed in existing technology, which matches how this thesis originated, from the Engineering BU's stated difficulty in Section 1.2, rather than from a novel algorithmic technique in search of an application.

Within the design and development step, CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) [@chapman2000crispdm] provides the specific process model this thesis follows for the data-and-modelling portion of the work. CRISP-DM structures a data mining project into six phases, business understanding, data understanding, data preparation, modelling, evaluation, and deployment, applied iteratively rather than strictly linearly. Schröer et al.'s systematic review of CRISP-DM applications [@schroer2021systematic] finds it remains the de facto standard process model across a wide range of application domains more than two decades after its publication, which supports treating it as the appropriate choice here rather than a more recent alternative; their review also notes that most published applications adapt CRISP-DM's phases to their specific domain rather than applying it unmodified, which this thesis does explicitly in Chapter 3 by mapping each CRISP-DM phase onto a concrete, named stage of the pipeline described in Chapter 4.

Using DSR and CRISP-DM together, as this thesis does, is a natural pairing rather than a redundant one: DSR provides the outer research framework that justifies building an artefact and specifies how it must be evaluated as a contribution to knowledge, while CRISP-DM provides the inner, domain-specific process discipline for the data mining work that sits inside that artefact's "design and development" step. Chapter 3 sets out exactly how the two are nested in this thesis's own process.

## Synthesis and Identified Gap

Reading these four bodies of work together situates this thesis precisely. The sales- and tendering-prediction literature [@yan2015sales; @zahid2021predicting; @rezazadeh2020generalized] establishes that win-probability prediction from historical opportunity data is a tractable, previously validated problem class, but every published instance works with confidential, unpublished organisational data and, as far as this review found, none addresses the video game co-development industry specifically. The tree-ensemble literature [@breiman2001random; @friedman2001greedy; @chen2016xgboost] establishes Random Forest and gradient boosting as the appropriate default model family for this kind of tabular, mixed-type, interaction-rich feature set, without settling which specific variant is best in advance of testing on the data at hand. The explainability literature [@lundberg2017unified; @olan2024enabling] establishes SHAP as a credible, increasingly adopted mechanism for making a business-facing model's output actionable, but has not, to this review's knowledge, been applied to compare a trained model's findings systematically against a business unit's own documented a priori hypotheses, which is precisely what RQ1 does. The methodology literature [@hevner2004design; @peffers2007design; @chapman2000crispdm; @schroer2021systematic] provides a well-established process for building and evaluating exactly this kind of artefact, but offers no domain-specific guidance for proposal bidding in creative-industry outsourcing.

The gap this thesis addresses, then, is not a gap in any one of these four literatures individually, each is mature on its own terms, but the absence of their combination applied to this specific, previously unstudied domain: a DSR-grounded, CRISP-DM-structured pipeline that predicts bid competitiveness for video game co-development proposals, uses SHAP both to explain individual predictions and to systematically check the model against the Engineering BU's own stated assumptions, and packages the result as a working, demonstrable artefact rather than a modelling exercise reported only in tables. Chapter 3 now turns to the methodology used to fill that gap.
