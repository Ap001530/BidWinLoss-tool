# Methodology

This chapter sets out how the thesis was carried out: the Design Science Research process followed, how CRISP-DM structures the data-and-modelling work inside that process, the full methodology and justification for the synthetic dataset the pipeline is trained and evaluated on, and a detailed account of the three scoping decisions introduced briefly in Section 1.6. The chapter closes with the tools and technologies used and a statement of the evaluation approach that Chapter 5 then applies.

## Overarching Framework: Design Science Research

This thesis follows the six-step Design Science Research process set out by Peffers et al. [@peffers2007design], entered at the problem-centred initiation point since the work began from a stated organisational problem (Section 1.2) rather than from a technology opportunity. Table 3.1 maps each DSR step to where it is addressed in this thesis.

| DSR step (Peffers et al. 2007) | How it is addressed | Thesis location |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Problem identification and motivation | The Engineering BU's manual, inconsistent bid-pursuit and pricing process, and the gap between resolved bid history and systematic use of it | Section 1.1, 1.2 |
| 2. Definition of objectives for a solution | Five concrete objectives: feature identification, CRISP-DM pipeline, SHAP explanation, hypothesis comparison, interactive artefact | Section 1.3 |
| 3. Design and development | Synthetic data generation, feature engineering, model training, SHAP pipeline, dashboard and scorer construction | This chapter; Chapter 4 |
| 4. Demonstration | The working dashboard and scorer, exercised against realistic example bids | Chapter 4, Section 4.4 |
| 5. Evaluation | Quantitative model performance, SHAP-vs-hypothesis comparison, qualitative artefact assessment against RQ3 | Chapter 5 |
| 6. Communication | This thesis document and the published, shareable artefact | Throughout; artefact links in Chapter 4 |

Hevner et al.'s [@hevner2004design] guideline that the research process itself be rigorously and repeatably documented (their Guideline 7) is addressed concretely by making every stage of the pipeline, from the synthetic data generator through to the published dashboard, available as runnable, version-controlled source code rather than described only in prose; Appendix C lists the complete set of scripts and where each DSR and CRISP-DM step is implemented.

## The Data Mining Process: CRISP-DM

Within the "design and development" DSR step, the data-and-modelling portion of the work follows CRISP-DM [@chapman2000crispdm], adapted to this project in the manner Schröer et al. [@schroer2021systematic] find typical of published CRISP-DM applications: the six phases are followed in substance, iteratively rather than strictly linearly, with each phase mapped onto a concrete, named artefact rather than treated as an abstract checklist item.

**Business understanding.** The business problem (Section 1.2) was translated into a supervised binary classification problem: given a bid's features at the time it is submitted, predict whether the outcome will be Won or Lost. The Engineering BU's documented feature hypotheses, its own prior, qualitative beliefs about what makes a bid competitive, were treated as the business-understanding artefact this phase is meant to produce, and are used twice downstream: first as the basis for the synthetic data generator's encoded relationships (next section), and second as the comparison point for the trained model's SHAP ranking (RQ1).

**Data understanding.** Because no real, resolved bid data was available (Section 1.6), this phase consisted of specifying the feature set itself, thirteen fields spanning client relationship history, deal origin, pre-proposal engagement, incumbency position, effort, price position, requirement coverage, portfolio fit, geographic/timezone fit, security credentials, and the number of competing bidders, and establishing plausible marginal distributions and realistic missingness patterns for each, so that the resulting dataset would read as a plausible bid log rather than an obviously synthetic grid of round numbers.

**Data preparation.** The thirteen features were encoded for modelling (one-hot encoding for categorical fields, median imputation for missing numeric values, most-frequent imputation for missing categorical values), and the dataset was split 75/25 into training and held-out test sets, stratified on the outcome label to preserve the roughly 30% win rate in both splits (Chapter 4, Section 4.2 gives full implementation detail).

**Modelling.** A gradient-boosted tree ensemble (scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingClassifier`, implementing Friedman's [-@friedman2001greedy] algorithm) was trained on the prepared training data, with hyperparameters (tree depth, learning rate, minimum samples per leaf, subsampling rate) selected to balance fit against the overfitting risk that a dataset of 500 rows and thirteen features otherwise carries. A second, linear logistic-regression model was also trained on the full dataset specifically to support the artefact's interactive scorer; Section 3.4 explains why a second model was needed for that component rather than reusing the gradient-boosted model directly.

**Evaluation.** The gradient-boosted model was evaluated on the held-out test set using AUC, the Brier score, and a confusion matrix at the default 0.5 threshold, and separately using 5-fold stratified cross-validation across the full dataset for a more stable accuracy estimate. SHAP values were computed for every bid in the dataset and aggregated into a global feature-importance ranking, which was then compared quantitatively (via Spearman rank correlation) against the BU's documented hypothesis ranking. Full results are reported in Chapter 5.

**Deployment.** The trained model's output, performance metrics, SHAP values, the hypothesis comparison, and the logistic surrogate's coefficients, was exported as a single structured results file and used to drive the interactive dashboard and scorer described in Chapter 4, published as a shareable web artefact rather than deployed into a live production system, consistent with this thesis's proof-of-concept scope (Section 1.6).

## Synthetic Data: Full Methodology and Justification

No empirical, resolved Virtuos bid records were available at the time this thesis was carried out. Rather than treat this as a blocking constraint, the data generation step was designed as a deliberate validation exercise: a synthetic dataset in which the relationships between features and the Won/Lost outcome are *encoded by the author*, derived from the Engineering BU's documented feature hypotheses rather than from the author's own preference or from any real practitioner data, so that the entire downstream pipeline, modelling, SHAP, hypothesis comparison, artefact, can be exercised and checked against a known, recoverable signal before any real data is available.

The claim this validates is narrow and stated explicitly: *the pipeline correctly recovers feature-outcome relationships that were encoded from the Engineering BU's documented hypotheses, under realistic noise*. Because the data generator's weights come from the same hypotheses the trained model's SHAP ranking is later compared against, agreement between the two is expected by construction and is not independent evidence about real Virtuos bids; it is evidence that the *method* works. This distinction is maintained throughout this thesis and is repeated here because it is the single most important methodological caveat in the entire study.

### Design Principles Against Trivial Circularity

A generator that encodes a clean, deterministic rule (for example, "win if and only if price is below benchmark and relationship history is positive") would let any model, including a trivial one, achieve near-perfect accuracy, which would demonstrate nothing about the pipeline's ability to recover a *realistically noisy* signal. Six design principles were applied specifically to avoid this:

1. **Noisy logistic outcome.** The win probability for each bid is a logistic function of a latent score, computed as a weighted sum of encoded feature effects, plus independent Gaussian noise (σ ≈ 1.25) added to that latent score before the logistic transform. The model therefore cannot achieve near-100% accuracy even if it recovers every encoded relationship exactly.
2. **Label noise.** Approximately 5% of outcomes are flipped after the logistic draw, mimicking the mislabelled or genuinely surprising results that occur in real bid logs (an unusually priced bid that wins anyway, a strong proposal lost to an unmodelled factor).
3. **Correlated features.** Features are drawn with realistic correlations rather than independently: inbound deal origin is more likely given a prior good relationship; pre-proposal engagement is more likely given either inbound origin or a prior good relationship; price position is correlated with project effort (larger engagements receive a modest discount).
4. **Interaction effects.** Three non-additive interactions are deliberately encoded: a weak portfolio match penalises a brand-new client far more than a repeat client; security credentials matter substantially more for AAA-publisher and platform-holder clients than for other segments; and indie-studio clients are more price-sensitive, so overpricing carries an extra penalty specific to that segment. A purely linear model cannot fully capture these, which motivates the use of a tree-based learner (Section 2.2) rather than only a linear baseline.
5. **Class imbalance.** The intercept of the logistic model is calibrated so the overall win rate lands close to 30%, matching a realistic bid log rather than an artificially balanced 50/50 split, and forcing the pipeline to handle the imbalance explicitly in its evaluation (Section 2.1, He and Garcia [@he2009learning]) rather than benefiting from a favourable class balance it would not have on real data.
6. **Missing values.** Values are set to missing at random for three fields (pre-proposal engagement, requirement coverage, and number of competing bidders) at rates of 5 to 8%, so the pipeline's imputation step is genuinely exercised rather than trivially unnecessary.

### A Deliberately Noteworthy Encoded Pattern

One relationship is encoded counter-intuitively on purpose, to give the pipeline something genuinely non-obvious to recover rather than only confirming an expected monotonic pattern. Relationship history is modelled on a four-point ordinal scale (new client; prior client whose delivery went poorly; prior client whose delivery was neutral; prior client whose delivery went well), and the "prior client, delivery went poorly" level is encoded as *worse* for win probability than a brand-new client, not simply less good than a positive prior relationship. A model, and a SHAP analysis, that correctly separates "prior, poor delivery" from "prior, good delivery" rather than collapsing all repeat business into a single positive effect demonstrates that the pipeline captures a nuance a naive binary "is this a repeat client?" feature would miss entirely. Chapter 5 reports whether this pattern was in fact recovered.

### Feature Set

Because synthetic generation removes the constraint of "was this ever actually logged," the feature set used is somewhat richer than a BU's real historical records might currently capture. Thirteen features were generated: client segment, project type, relationship history (the four-point ordinal scale above, with prior delivery outcome folded in), deal origin (inbound or outbound), pre-proposal engagement, incumbency position (greenfield, defending existing work, or displacing a competitor), effort in person-months, price position relative to benchmark, requirement coverage, portfolio similarity, geographic/timezone fit, security credentials, and the number of competing bidders. The last of these, number of competing bidders, carries an explicit caveat that is maintained throughout this thesis: in a real deployment, this figure is frequently known only *after* the bid decision is made, which makes it a leakage risk if it were available to the model at training time but not at genuine prediction time. It is retained here for completeness, since the full set of the BU's documented hypotheses includes it, but is flagged wherever it appears, and a real-data implementation of this pipeline should either verify it is genuinely known at bid time or demote it to analysis-only use (Chapter 7).

### Validation That the Data Is Non-Trivial

Before any downstream modelling, a simple logistic-regression ceiling check was run directly on the generated data: a 5-fold cross-validated logistic regression achieves an AUC of approximately 0.72 on this dataset. This figure matters as a sanity check in both directions. An AUC far below this (near 0.5) would indicate the encoded signal was too weak or too noisy to be learnable at all; an AUC very close to 1.0 would indicate the data was trivially circular, learnable by any model regardless of its capability. An AUC around 0.7 to 0.75 for a simple linear baseline indicates a dataset with real, recoverable signal that is neither trivial nor unlearnable, exactly the design goal. Chapter 5 compares the gradient-boosted model's own performance against this baseline.

## The Three Scoping Deviations, in Full

Section 1.6 stated three scoping decisions briefly; this section gives the full reasoning for the two that represent deliberate deviations from the thesis's original proposal.

### Deviation 1: A Single Gradient-Boosted Model Instead of a Random Forest-versus-XGBoost Comparison

The original proposal specified comparing Random Forest and XGBoost as candidate classifiers (RQ2). The delivered pipeline instead trains and evaluates a single gradient-boosted ensemble, scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingClassifier`, a direct implementation of Friedman's [-@friedman2001greedy] algorithm and a close methodological relative of XGBoost [@chen2016xgboost] without that library's additional engineering optimisations. Three considerations drove this narrowing. First, proportionate effort: a rigorous Random Forest-versus-XGBoost comparison, properly tuned and validated, is itself a substantial piece of modelling work, and given the single-semester scope of this thesis, that effort was judged better spent on the SHAP explanation pipeline and the interactive artefact, which directly address RQ1 and RQ3 and were judged more central to the thesis's DSR contribution (an explainable, demonstrable decision-support tool) than an algorithm bake-off would be. Second, the literature reviewed in Section 2.2 indicates that Random Forest and gradient-boosted variants typically perform comparably on tabular data of this size and type, with the gap between them generally smaller than the variance introduced by dataset size, noise, and feature engineering choices; the specific choice of ensemble family was judged less consequential to this thesis's research questions than the explainability and artefact-design work that those questions actually hinge on. Third, SHAP's TreeSHAP algorithm [@lundberg2017unified] applies natively and efficiently to gradient-boosted trees, which kept the explanation pipeline (central to RQ1 and RQ3) straightforward to implement correctly.

This is treated as a limitation, not a methodological equivalence claim: RQ2 as stated in Section 1.4 asks about classification accuracy and robustness under CRISP-DM generally, not about any one named algorithm, so the research question itself is still answered by the work as delivered, but the answer is narrower than originally scoped. Chapter 6 revisits this as a threat to validity, and Chapter 7 identifies the comparison as the single most valuable, well-defined piece of future work, precisely because the synthetic dataset and evaluation harness built for this thesis already make that comparison straightforward to run.

### Deviation 2: An Interactive Web Dashboard Instead of Power BI

The original proposal specified Power BI, refreshed through an MLOps pipeline using Power BI's APIs, as the artefact's delivery mechanism. The delivered artefact is an interactive web dashboard, built as self-contained HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with D3.js for charting, published both as a shareable Claude Artifact and as a static site. This substitution was made for three reasons, each with a direct bearing on RQ3's requirement that the artefact be demonstrable and usable.

First, access. A Power BI dashboard requires reviewers, a thesis committee, and the Engineering BU's own stakeholders to have Power BI Desktop or an appropriately licensed Power BI Service account to open it at all; a web dashboard requires only a browser, which removes a real access barrier for anyone evaluating or using the artefact, including during the thesis defence itself.

Second, exactness of the interactive scorer. RQ3 calls for a what-if scorer a stakeholder can use directly, and the design goal for that scorer (Section 4.4) was that every factor it displays be an *exact*, not approximated, contribution to the predicted probability, computed live as the user adjusts a bid's features. A Power BI dashboard built against a scheduled data refresh is well suited to displaying precomputed model output, but is not naturally suited to running a live, client-side probability calculation in response to user input; building the scorer as a logistic-regression surrogate evaluated directly in JavaScript (Section 4.4 explains the surrogate-model choice) was more straightforward and more exactly auditable as a web artefact than as a Power BI report.

Third, iteration speed. Building the pipeline, the artefact, and this thesis in parallel over a single semester favoured a tool with a short edit-preview-publish cycle; the web dashboard could be revised and republished within the same working session the underlying model or data changed, without a separate data-refresh or publish step.

The resulting trade-off is real and is stated plainly rather than minimised: Power BI's native enterprise deployment model, including row-level security, scheduled automatic refresh against a live data source, and integration into an organisation's existing Power BI workspace alongside other BU reporting, is not demonstrated by this artefact. Chapter 6 discusses this trade-off as a limitation, and Appendix B sets out concretely what porting the current pipeline's output (the structured results file described in the Deployment phase above) to a Power BI report would involve, since the pipeline was deliberately built to produce that output as a portable, self-contained file rather than in a format tied to the web dashboard specifically.

### Scoping Decision 3: Single Business Unit, Proof-of-Concept Maturity

The third scoping point from Section 1.6, restated here for completeness rather than as a deviation, is that the thesis is deliberately bounded to a single business unit within a single organisation and to proof-of-concept artefact maturity. Production concerns, including authentication, audit logging, integration with a live CRM or bid-tracking system, and ongoing model retraining and monitoring, are explicitly out of scope. This is not a deviation from the original proposal, which was itself scoped to the Engineering BU, but is stated here because it bears directly on how Chapter 5's evaluation and Chapter 7's future-work recommendations should be read: this thesis evaluates whether the pipeline and artefact *work correctly as designed*, not whether they are production-ready.

## Tools and Technologies

The pipeline and artefact were built with Python 3 for data generation and modelling (NumPy and pandas for data handling, scikit-learn for the gradient-boosted and logistic-regression models and for cross-validation and preprocessing utilities, and the `shap` package for TreeSHAP explanation), Git for version control of every stage of the pipeline and of this thesis document itself, and HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and D3.js for the interactive dashboard and scorer. Pandoc, driven from Markdown chapter files and a BibTeX reference list, was used to produce this thesis document, so that chapter text, like the pipeline code, is version-controlled and diffable rather than held only in binary form.

The static figures reproduced in this thesis (Chapters 4 and 5) are generated by a dedicated script, `thesis/make_figures.py`, using Matplotlib, rather than drawn by hand or exported once and then edited. This script reads `outputs/model_results.json`, the same file the pipeline's interactive dashboard reads from, so a figure in this thesis and the corresponding chart a reader sees in the live dashboard are two renderings of the same underlying numbers rather than independently maintained artefacts that could silently drift apart after a future pipeline run. This follows the same reproducibility principle Hevner et al.'s [-@hevner2004design] seventh guideline calls for (Section 3.1): re-running `python3 thesis/make_figures.py` after any change to the model or the data regenerates every figure in this thesis from the updated results automatically, with no manual charting step for a future author or examiner to repeat by hand.

## Evaluation Approach

Chapter 5 evaluates the delivered pipeline and artefact along the two tracks the DSR framework above calls for: quantitative evaluation of the predictive model (AUC on a held-out test set and via 5-fold cross-validation, the Brier score as a calibration measure, and precision, recall, and F1 at the default classification threshold, each read against the class-imbalance caveat from Section 2.1), and a combined quantitative and qualitative evaluation of the explanation and artefact components (the Spearman rank correlation between the SHAP-derived and BU-hypothesis feature rankings, with specific attention to where the two diverge and why, and a structured walkthrough of the dashboard and scorer against what RQ3 requires of them). Both tracks are evaluated against the synthetic dataset's own ceiling (the logistic-regression baseline AUC established in this chapter), not against an assumed external benchmark, consistent with this thesis's stated claim that it validates a method rather than reports a finding about real bids.
